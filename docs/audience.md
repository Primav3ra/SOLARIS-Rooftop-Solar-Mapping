# Audience analysis and writing standard

Written for: whoever edits copy on this site next.

This document exists because the site's prose drifted into an explanatory,
self-referential register — captions that told the reader which chart mattered
most, and adjectives grading the project's own components. That register is
wrong for every audience below. This page records who those audiences are, what
they arrive needing, and the writing rules that follow.

## Audiences

Four groups, in descending order of expected volume.

### 1. Prospective rooftop adopter

A building owner or tenant evaluating a rooftop installation.

**Arrives with** a vendor quote or a general intention, and no way to test
either.

**Pain points.** Vendor estimates are typically generated from roof area and a
regional irradiance figure, with losses represented by a single performance
ratio. They do not distinguish a roof overshadowed on its south side from an
unobstructed one, and they do not vary with local dust or cleaning practice. The
adopter has no independent means of checking the figure and no basis for
negotiating it.

**Needs.** An independent estimate with an explicit range; identification of
which loss term dominates at their location; and a statement of what a site
survey must still establish. The dominant loss term is the actionable output —
soiling implies a cleaning schedule, sky obstruction does not.

### 2. Installer or EPC estimator

**Arrives** needing to triage leads before committing to a site visit.

**Pain points.** Site visits carry a fixed cost per lead. Pre-visit screening
normally relies on satellite imagery inspected by eye, which resolves footprint
but not height, and therefore not inter-building shading.

**Needs.** Specific yield in the units used in commercial practice
(kWh/kWp/yr), the loss decomposition, and an unambiguous list of what the model
does not cover so the survey scope is clear. Comparability between candidate
sites matters more than absolute accuracy.

### 3. Municipal or programme analyst

**Arrives** sizing rooftop potential across a ward, city or scheme.

**Pain points.** Aggregate potential estimates are commonly produced by
multiplying built-up area by a capacity density, which discards all spatial
variation and cannot be defended in review.

**Needs.** Method provenance with citations, the reference data and its
disagreement, and spatial variation that can be attributed to a cause.

### 4. Technical reviewer

An examiner, supervisor or engineer assessing the method.

**Needs.** Validation design, holdout construction, the gate declared before
model selection, and the failure history. This group reads the limitations page
first.

## What all four need and the site must supply

1. **Uncertainty attached to the number, not filed separately.** The two free
   reference datasets for India differ by 10.6%; no output can be presented as
   tighter than that.
2. **Attribution of loss to cause.** A single net figure is not actionable. The
   decomposition is the product.
3. **An explicit scope boundary.** Screening estimate, not engineering
   estimate. Stated wherever a figure is presented, not only on one page.
4. **Provenance.** Every coefficient traceable to a citation; every reported
   figure traceable to a committed artifact.

## Writing standard

**Register.** Declarative and quantitative. Report what was measured and under
what conditions.

**Prohibited.** Instructions about salience ("the first thing to know", "the
single most useful thing here"); evaluative grading of the project's own
components ("the least defensible part"); rhetorical contrast as a substitute
for evidence ("not X, it is Y"); and narration of the site's own structure.

**Quantify.** Prefer "losses total 5–12% of baseline irradiance" to "most of the
energy survives". Where a figure is a range across the evaluation set, give the
range.

**Vary paragraph length.** A single-sentence paragraph is the correct form for a
finding. Uniform six-line blocks read as generated text and flatten the
distinction between a result and its context.

**Captions state what is plotted and what conditions the reading.** They do not
instruct interpretation. A caption may state a sensitivity — "ranking is
sensitive to the beam fraction below approximately 0.5" — because that is a
property of the data.

**Attribute limitations to mechanism, not to sentiment.** "SRTM resolves terrain
at 30 m, so the slope exclusion operates on terrain rather than roof pitch" is a
mechanism. "This is a weakness we are honest about" is not.
